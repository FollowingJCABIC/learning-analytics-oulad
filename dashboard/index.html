Learning Analytics Project

Understanding Student Engagement, Assessments, and Course Outcomes

This project uses anonymized historical online-course data to study how recorded platform activity and assessment behavior relate to course outcomes. It also tests whether information available during a course can help estimate the risk of a poor result on the next assessment.

This is a historical research and portfolio project. It does not monitor current students, recommend interventions, or make decisions about individuals.

Why this matters

Online-course data is easy to count and easy to misread

Learning platforms create detailed records, but a click is not the same as learning and a forecast is not the same as an explanation. This project shows how those records can be organized into useful historical evidence while keeping context, uncertainty, and responsible limits visible.

What this project asks

Five questions guide the analysis

These questions organize the charts that follow, moving from description to forecasting without treating a statistical association as a cause.

  1. How did recorded online activity change across the course?
  2. How did missing assessments and submission timing differ across courses?
  3. What did recorded activity look like near withdrawal?
  4. Could information available so far help forecast the next assessment?
  5. How reliable were the model's rankings and predicted probabilities?

The historical data

What is OULAD?

The Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD) is an anonymized historical dataset containing course information, assessment records, recorded student outcomes, and activity on an online learning platform. It covers selected Open University course offerings from 2013 and 2014.

What I found

The main findings in one minute

Each statement below comes from the downloaded archive, PostgreSQL analysis tables, or saved held-out model results.

  • Withdrawal was a substantial recorded outcome. 10,156 of 32,593 attempts, or 31.2%, ended in withdrawal. The data does not record every reason why.
  • Recorded activity changed over the course. The typical active-record median peaked at 70 clicks in week 2 and was 18.5 by week 12.
  • Missing assessments varied greatly by course offering. Observed rates ranged from 14.7% to 56.5%, so one universal missing-work rule would ignore course context.
  • Recorded activity became quieter near withdrawal. Median clicks moved from 26 twelve weeks before unregistration to 10 one week before it.
  • The assessment forecast found some useful signal. The interpretable calibrated model reached 0.594 precision-recall AUC against a 0.211 prevalence baseline.
  • The forecast remained uncertain and context-specific. Probability errors remained, performance changed by week, and results may not transfer to another institution or period.

The descriptive story

What the historical records show

Each chart answers one project question. The takeaway appears first, followed by what was measured and the most important interpretation limit.

Course outcomes

How often did each recorded course outcome occur?

Withdrawal accounted for 31.2% of course attempts, so it was not a rare edge case in these records.

The bars count one outcome for each course attempt: 3,024 distinction, 12,361 pass, 7,052 fail, and 10,156 withdrawal. A course attempt is one student taking one course offering, not necessarily one unique person.

Keep in mind: The chart reports historical outcomes. It does not explain why a course attempt ended in withdrawal, failure, or success.

Weekly online activity

How did recorded platform activity change across the term?

The typical weekly median rose from 46 clicks in week 0 to 70 in week 2, then fell to 18.5 by week 12.

The median represents a typical active record. The upper quartile represents a comparatively active portion of the records and remained at 41.5 clicks in week 12. The gap shows why one average cannot describe everyone.

Keep in mind: This chart summarizes active records. The forecasting data also retains weeks with zero recorded activity so those weeks are not silently dropped. Clicks indicate platform use, not attention, understanding, motivation, or learning.

Technical note

The plotted lines are medians of module-presentation summaries. The forecasting table uses one row per student attempt and course week, including explicit zero-activity weeks.

Activity consistency

Why separate activity volume from consistency?

Similar activity volumes appear across very different numbers of active weeks, and the recorded outcome groups overlap heavily.

Two students can produce a similar number of clicks per active week while one appears in many more course weeks. Volume and consistency describe different aspects of the historical record, so the analysis retains both.

Keep in mind: Neither axis measures learning quality, and neither measure alone cleanly separates the four recorded course outcomes.

Missing assessments

How much did missing assessment records differ by course?

The percentage ranged from 14.7% in AAA 2013J to 56.5% in CCC 2014B.

Each bar is the percentage of eligible student-assessment pairs without a recorded submission in one course offering. The wide range is why the analysis keeps course context rather than using one universal missing-work rule.

Keep in mind: Differences may reflect course structure, assessment design, deadlines, extensions, recording practices, and circumstances not represented in the data. Missing work is not proof of low effort.

Submission timing

Were assessments usually recorded before or after the deadline?

Typical timing ranged from 14 days early in GGG 2013J to 2 days late in the latest-recorded offerings.

A negative value means the median submission was recorded before the due date. A positive value means it was recorded after the due date. Zero means the typical recorded submission occurred on the deadline.

Keep in mind: The chart does not capture every extension, local policy, or personal circumstance that may have affected timing.

Withdrawal timing

What did recorded activity look like near withdrawal?

Median weekly clicks moved from 26 twelve weeks before unregistration to 10 in the week immediately before it.

The chart aligns each withdrawing course attempt around the week in which unregistration was recorded. This makes relative timing comparable even when calendar dates differ.

Keep in mind: The pattern cannot explain why someone withdrew. Academic, financial, employment, family, health, and other circumstances may be absent from OULAD, and the number of observable attempts changes across relative weeks.

Forecasting

Can course information available so far help forecast the next assessment?

The historical records contained some useful predictive information, but the forecast remained uncertain. It should be treated as an analytical demonstration, not a diagnosis or an automated decision about an individual.

What exactly is being forecast?

At the end of each course week, the model estimates whether the next non-exam assessment will be missing by its due date or will receive a recorded score below 40. Each weekly snapshot represents what would have been known by the end of that week. Later activity and future assessment results are excluded.

01 Learn from 2013

The models were trained using earlier course offerings.

02 Choose with 2014B

A separate group supported model selection and probability calibration.

03 Test on unseen 2014J

Complete later offerings were held back for the final evaluation.

Weekly records from the same course offering were kept together instead of being randomly scattered across training and testing. This temporal held-out design reduces the chance that closely related records appear on both sides of the evaluation.

0.594 Precision-recall AUC

Summarizes how well the model ranks upcoming negative assessment events above other records. Higher is better; 1.0 is perfect. The held-out event prevalence baseline was 0.211.

0.130 Brier score

Measures how close predicted probabilities were to what happened. Lower is better; 0 represents perfect probability predictions.

Model comparison

Did the historical records contain predictive information?

The calibrated logistic model reached 0.594 precision-recall AUC, compared with an event prevalence baseline of 0.211.

The gradient-boosted challenger ranked highest at 0.642, while the calibrated logistic model remains the interpretable probability reference. The later 2014J course offerings were unseen during model training.

Keep in mind: The result shows some predictive information, not certainty, causation, or deployment readiness. It is not a diagnosis of a student.

Technical note

Precision-recall AUC summarizes how effectively a model ranks records with an upcoming event above records without one. A score of 1.0 is perfect; event prevalence supplies a useful baseline.

Probability quality

Did predicted probabilities resemble what happened?

Predicted and observed rates were close in lower-risk groups, while the highest-risk group was overestimated.

In the highest probability group, the mean prediction was 85% and the observed event rate was 70%. Calibration asks whether a predicted 30% risk corresponds to an event rate near 30% among similar records.

Keep in mind: A model can rank records reasonably well while still producing imperfect probabilities. Ranking and probability accuracy are different evaluation questions.

Technical note

The calibrated logistic model's Brier score is 0.130. Brier score measures squared error between predicted probabilities and outcomes; lower is better and 0 is perfect.

Decision thresholds

What changes when the probability cutoff moves?

Flagging more records finds more eventual events, but it also creates more false alarms.

At a 50% cutoff, 16,901 of 117,186 weekly records were flagged (14.4%). Precision was 61.6%, recall was 42.1%, and true alerts had a median 26 days of lead time.

Keep in mind: There is no universally correct threshold. The appropriate cutoff depends on purpose, resources, consequences of errors, and whether flagging would actually help.

Technical note

Precision is the share of flags that became events. Recall is the share of all events that were flagged. A false alarm is a flag without the event; a missed case is an event below the cutoff.

Forecast timing

How did forecast quality change as more history became available?

Later weeks generally provided better ranking, but a later forecast also leaves less time for any useful response.

Early in a course, the model has relatively little activity and assessment history. Later weekly records contain a longer history, which can improve the forecast while reducing lead time.

Keep in mind: The chart describes held-out 2014J offerings only. Week-specific performance may change in another institution, period, or course design.

Responsible interpretation

What this analysis cannot tell us

Clicks are traces, not explanations. The project preserves uncertainty because the records omit much of the context needed to understand any individual experience.

  • The anonymized data covers selected Open University courses from 2013-2014.
  • Platform clicks do not directly measure learning, attention, or motivation.
  • Students may study offline or use resources not represented in the records.
  • Reasons for withdrawal are not fully represented in the dataset.
  • Course differences may reflect design, deadlines, policy, or recording practice.
  • Historical associations do not establish that one behavior caused an outcome.
  • Results may not generalize to other years, institutions, or course structures.
  • The forecasts should not be used as an automated decision system.
  • Predictions are not statements about character, intelligence, motivation, ability, or potential.
  • Fairness in another setting would require local validation, governance, and meaningful participation from affected people.

Professional relevance

What this project demonstrates

The value of the project is not only the final model. It is the complete, reviewable process from a large relational source to a careful public explanation.

  • Translating a complex relational dataset into usable analytical tables
  • Designing reproducible PostgreSQL and SQL data pipelines
  • Conducting exploratory and statistical analysis
  • Creating weekly, time-aware forecasting features
  • Preventing future information from leaking into model training
  • Evaluating ranking, probability quality, thresholds, and timing
  • Communicating uncertainty, responsible-use limits, and negative results
  • Presenting one analysis to both technical and nontechnical audiences
